## 1. Seven patterns almost every growth portfolio shares

1. **The number shows up before the reader scrolls.** It sits in the title (Jessica), in a big stat band near the top (JP: "27% / 32% / $135M"), or in "Impact Overview" cards under the hero image (Ben). Only the slide deck saves the number for the end.
2. **An "at a glance" block sits under the title**: role, team, timeline, company. Ben shows team members as avatar chips ("Me PM EM Data UR FE FE BE"). JP adds "OPPORTUNITY SPACES: Activation · Conversion". Jessica adds "SITE REACH ~200k organic".
3. **Every project is placed on the funnel.** Acquisition, activation, conversion (free to paid), retention, monetization. JP tags each experiment card "ACTIVATION" or "CONVERSION".
4. **There is a baseline, then a change.** "activation rate from 3% to 12.7%" (Ben). "Drop-off in onboarding was too high, upwards of 25%" (Jessica). A funnel table with step rates of 35.3% vs 37.3% (Sergeychik).
5. **The hypothesis is stated in plain words.** Jessica: *"Design hypothesis: If we reduce friction upfront and give users more context before asking them to commit, more of them will convert."* Sergeychik: *"A guided, one-page checkout will reduce drop-off and increase conversion."*
6. **Control vs. variation is shown side by side.** Jessica labels the two screenshots "Control" and "Variation" and rings the one thing that changed (`growth-refs/jessicagoldman-cs-5.jpg`). Ben uses an "Old / New" toggle with two metric cards under it.
7. **They are short.** About 570–1,800 words, in paragraphs of 1–3 sentences. Leslie Yang's rule: tell each section in "3–5 sentences (not multiple paragraphs)."

### What nobody shows, and how you can stand out

No portfolio we read shows a **losing test**, names **guardrail metrics**, or says **what they'd test next**. Adobe's growth leads list those as the core of the job:

> "significant wins and significant losses mean you've done your job well." — Verna Swehla, Adobe Express growth design lead
>
> "I fail a lot. About 40 percent of the time… failure is part of my job as a growth designer." — Jessica Kende, Adobe

You already have this story. In your Windows search tests, the leading concept changed once you asked people *where they expected the result to open*. Tell it the way Kende tells hers: hypothesis, result, why it happened, what changed next. That puts you ahead of every portfolio above.

## 2. Three case study structures to choose from

### A. The experiment log (JP Holdsworth, Calendly)

Best when you ran several tests on one surface.

1. Eyebrow: `№ 04 / SELECTED WORK · CALENDLY · SENIOR PRODUCT DESIGNER, GROWTH · 2021`
2. Title plus a one-line subtitle naming the team and its remit
3. **01 / At a glance**: role, timeline, team, company, opportunity spaces
4. **The team**: 2 paragraphs on why growth design was needed and how the team worked
5. **Stat band**: 3 big numbers, each with a label and sub-label
6. **02 / The experiments**: 6 numbered cards. Each card is 2 sentences of problem or hypothesis, one shipped screen, and a `RESULT` line: *"+7.36% conversion rate at 95% statistical significance."*

His working method fits in one sentence: *"research a friction point → design against the finding → ship in experiment → read the data → iterate."*

### B. The research-to-shipped story (Ben Shih, Lokalise)

Best for a single large redesign with several parts.

Title → meta block → hero image → **Impact Overview** (3 metric cards) → "Why we started" → "How I tackle the problem" (Step 1 dogfooding, Step 2 quant data, Step 3 interviews) → "3 major frictions" → design sprint and testing → "Final designs and what we shipped" (each piece paired with metric cards) → "Feedback & impact" (a recap list plus a user quote).

A sticky "ON THIS PAGE" menu on the left lets people jump straight to the results.

### C. The hypothesis-and-test story (Jessica Goldman, Gusto; Sergeychik, Adobe Checkout)

The classic growth shape, and the closest match to what interviewers ask about.

| # | Section | Jessica's heading | Sergeychik's label |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Context + audit | "UP FIRST… Audit the original flow and identify key pain points" | KEY PROBLEMS · BEFORE |
| 2 | Data | "Turning data into design opportunities" | DATA: "Combining steps led to a 7% lift in gross new orders." |
| 3 | Hypothesis + goal | "Design hypothesis:" / "Key design goals:" | HYPOTHESIS |
| 4 | Success metrics | target "boost lead capture by 5%+" | MEASURING SUCCESS: "Success, defined before we built." (PRIMARY / SECONDARY) |
| 5 | The design | "My 4-step solution" | Design principles, then the screens |
| 6 | The test | "The test: one change to the hero" → "Testing variations and learning fast" | n/a |
| 7 | Results | "Analyze impact": +15% / +25% / +1,000 | MVP OUTCOME: "3.8% Lift in order volume · 4.3% ARR increase · $7.3M Global ARR impact" |
| 8 | Learning | *"Adding friction to account creation while lowering the barrier to becoming a lead proved more effective…"* | Cross-team partnership, people management |

Diana Prohoda adds two useful devices: **decision cards** in a "Why? / The trade-off: / Result:" format, and a **benchmark vs. target table** for results she couldn't measure because she left before launch. She labels those numbers as projected, which is an honest way to handle work that never shipped.

## 3. The words they use

**Funnel stages:** acquisition, activation, "AHA moment" / "setup moment", first value, conversion, free-to-paid, trial-to-paid, retention (Day-1, D7, 30-day), repeat engagement, monetization, expansion

**Problems:** friction, friction point, drop-off, bottleneck, leak, "one-and-done", cognitive load, banner blindness

**Experiments:** hypothesis, control vs. variation (Adobe says "Control vs. Challenger"), A/B test, fake door test, lift, statistical significance, primary / secondary metrics, segment, "ship in experiment", "read the data", "flat or negative"

**Business:** ARR, LTV, ARPU, MQL pipeline, lead capture, gross new orders, "incremental ARR"

**Team:** pod, squad, "opportunity spaces", partnering with PM / Data / Research / Eng

Adobe sums up the mindset as *"hypothesis-based thinking (metrics and user behaviors)"* rather than *"requirements-based thinking."* Write your process in those terms: say "we believed X would change Y, measured by Z", not "the requirements were…".

They also make a point of saying they avoid dark patterns: *"meaningful growth happens when you inspire users, not convince them."* One sentence like that in your intro signals good judgment.

## 4. How they write paragraphs

- **Lead each paragraph with the claim, then give the proof.** *"In 2021, Calendly had millions of individual users and a growth surface that hadn't kept pace."* (JP)
- **Use 1–3 sentences per paragraph.** Jessica uses bold run-in labels to make sections scannable: **Problem:** / **What I did:** / **Why it worked:**
- **Say "I" for your decisions and "we" for team results.** Ben: *"As the founding designer of the growth team, I had the opportunity to establish the foundation of the design process."* Leslie Yang: *"Be clear about what you did versus other team members' contributions."*
- **Use short fragments for rhythm.** JP: *"No inherited playbook."* Sergeychik: *"One page. One goal."* and *"Too rigid and no one adopts it. Too loose and it stops being a platform."*
- **Give every result its scope.** A number comes with its population, timeframe and confidence: *"+7.1% second ET booked within 14 days for all users, +13% for new users."*
- **Section headings make claims.** Use *"We had 54 fragmented button styles. 🤯"* (Clements) or *"Success, defined before we built."* (Sergeychik), not "Research".
- **The tone is confident and plain.** Ben's is the most playful (*"Can you guess what those frictions were? 👇"*). That works on a personal site but is optional.

## 5. How it looks

| Element | What they do |
|---|---|
| Hero | Product mockup (Ben, Jessica), or giant display type with no image (JP, Sergeychik) |
| Metrics | Large numbers in an accent color (green for Ben and JP, pastel tiles for Jessica, white on black for Sergeychik), with a small label and sub-label underneath |
| Data visuals | Ben: funnel bar chart and an activation decline line. Sergeychik: funnel table. JP: numbers only, no charts |
| Test visuals | Labelled control/variation screenshots, with the changed element circled |
| Flows | Rows of 4 phones with arrows: the original flow, then "My 4-step solution" (Jessica) |
| Layout | Text column of ~700–720px. Images run wider (~880–1,120px). Roughly 50–60% image by area |
| Navigation | Sticky "On this page" menu (Ben); monospace "01 / …" section labels in a left gutter (JP) |
| Aesthetic | Mostly warm off-white or cream backgrounds, one accent color, an editorial grotesk or serif. Calm, not flashy |

**Homepages:**
- **Positioning line names growth outright.** JP: *"I design and develop products with an emphasis on activation and conversion."* Ben: *"Staff Product Designer with a Data Science background. Driving growth at Lovable by day…"*
- **Project cards carry the outcome**, either as a number in the sentence (JP: *"…took the business from $50M to $135M in ARR"*) or as three stat tiles (Clements).
- **Locked work is marked "Password required" right on the card** (Ben). The cards stay visible, which is useful for your Windows NDA work.

**Interview deck vs. site:** Leslie Yang recommends a separate deck for the interview, *"versus presenting directly from your site or design files"*:
- Two recent projects, about 20 minutes each, inside a 45-minute presentation plus 15 minutes of Q&A
- 2–3 bullets and 1–2 designs per slide
- End each project with *"What would you have done differently? What surprised you?"*

Sergeychik's slide-deck site shows what a growth deck looks like: one label, one headline sentence and one visual per slide (`growth-refs/sergeychik-*.jpg`).

## 6. A template for your growth case studies

```
[Eyebrow]  COMPANY · YOUR TITLE · FUNNEL STAGE · YEAR
[Title]    <Product>: <outcome with number> by <what you changed>
[Meta]     Role · Team (PM, Data, Research, Eng) · Timeline · Reach (users / traffic)
[Stats]    3 big numbers: primary metric, secondary metric, scale or business effect

01 The problem in the funnel   Where people dropped off, with a baseline. 2–3 sentences + funnel chart
02 What we learned             2–3 insights from data and research, each tied to a decision
03 Hypothesis                  "If we <change>, <users> will <behavior>, measured by <metric>."
                               Primary and guardrail metrics, defined before building
04 What I designed             Key screens; control vs. variation with the change circled
05 The tests                   Numbered cards: hypothesis → variant → RESULT line (lift, scope, confidence)
06 The one that lost           What you expected, what happened, why, and what you changed next
07 Impact                      Shipped results; what carried into the roadmap
08 What I'd test next          2–3 bullets
```

Aim for 700–1,200 words. Paragraphs should be 1–3 sentences. Give every section a visual.

## 7. Applied to your case studies

### Bing Real Estate: your strongest growth story

**Title options** (outcome first):
- "Bing Real Estate: +15% daily users by giving renters a reason to come back"
- "Growing a two-sided rental marketplace as its only designer"

**Read it through a growth lens**:

| Funnel stage | Your work | Metric you have |
|---|---|---|
| Retention / repeat engagement | Save Home + saved search: a reason to return | 103K homes saved |
| Supply-side acquisition | Owner claim-and-edit flow | 25K properties claimed, ~100 rental listings/week |
| Engagement overall | Features shipped across the vertical | +15% DAU |

**Still to find:**
- The DAU baseline and timeframe
- Whether Save Home or claim was A/B tested (or shipped in flights)
- Any guardrail, such as whether DSAT held
- Return rate of people who saved a home vs. people who didn't. Even a rough number would sharpen the story

Section 08, "What happened next", is your reflection section, which most growth portfolios skip.

### Windows search + Copilot: an experiment log, JP style

You ran 10 rounds of side-by-side tests in about a week, so use structure A. Number the test cards, give each a `RESULT` line (e.g. "Concept D won 54% of 600 judgments"), and make **Test 10 the "one that lost" moment**. Once you asked people where they expected a result to open, the leading concept changed (D fell to 39%, B rose to 57%).

Call these **concept tests** or **preference tests**, not A/B lifts. Growth interviewers will ask, and saying so up front builds trust. Keep the page password-protected, with the card visible on the homepage the way Ben does it.

### Salesforce Manage Users and Help: support deflection is a growth metric

"−32% support cases" and "97% leave Help without opening a case" are cost and retention outcomes. Put them in the title: *"Manage Users: 32% fewer support cases from a two-week MVP."*

### Homepage positioning

Draft lines in the style of JP and Ben:
- "Product designer who grows products by testing. +15% daily users on Bing Real Estate; Windows search and Copilot at Microsoft."
- "I design for activation and retention, then prove it with data. Currently at Microsoft (Bing, Windows, Edge)."
